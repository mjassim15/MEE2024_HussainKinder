Executive Summary

Three things to take away

The pipeline works

I adapted our MEE2024 Tab 1 centroid pipeline to PUNCH Level-3 white-light images. After three iterations, 86% of the 4,343 detected centroids correspond to real Gaia catalog stars.

PUNCH can't reach the signal

At ~81″/pixel, the gravitational deflection for even our closest detectable star is roughly 1,000× below the measured position noise — and moving closer to the Sun cannot fix it.

The machinery is the value

Centroid finding, catalog matching, and the sensitivity check are built and validated. The numbers tell us exactly what's needed next: a finer plate scale, closer to the Sun.

2Three iterations to a clean detection

MEE's built-in saturation mask assumes an over-exposed Sun. PUNCH's Sun is a dark hole, so that mask never activated — the corona flooded the detector with false positives until I masked it myself.

One important consequence: at 12 px per solar radius, a 1,000 px mask means the nearest star we can possibly detect sits at ~83 solar radii. That inner limit is self-imposed by the mask — not a physical property of the data.
Method

2The mask that made run 03 work

Every pixel within 1,000 px of Sun-center is replaced with a single flat sky value — the median of a thin ring just outside the mask — blanking 18.7% of the image before the centroid finder ever sees it.

Method

2Validating against Gaia

A detected centroid counts as a match if it lands within 2 pixels of where the Gaia catalog says a star should be, projected through PUNCH's pointing solution.

  • Purity = 86.3% — of the 4,343 things I flagged, 3,749 are real stars. The detector rarely cries wolf.
  • Completeness = 44.8% — of the 8,560 catalog stars bright enough to be in the usable field, I recovered about 45%. The fainter half stay below PUNCH's noise.
Validation

Would detecting stars closer to the Sun close the gap? No. Deflection grows as 1.75″/r, so to lift the signal above even a tenth of a pixel at PUNCH's scale, a star would need to sit at r < 0.2 solar radii — inside the Sun. The limitation is not where our stars are; it is that 81″ pixels are too coarse for this measurement at any radius.

3The evidence

That is a useful thing to know precisely — it points directly at what would work: the same pipeline on a coronagraph with a finer plate scale, which can both resolve smaller shifts and see stars much nearer the limb.

Limiting Result

4A visual-first check

One lesson from the first pass: I trusted the automated brightest-detections list without first confirming by eye which were actually stars. A conversation with Henry Throop (NASA) reinforced a better habit — inspect the frame visually and confirm star-like sources before matching.

The top-20 comparison was immediately informative: several of the brightest detections near the Sun have no catalog counterpart at all — they are corona structure, not stars — while the nearest visually confirmed star sits at ~98 solar radii.

(For inspection I use DS9, a standard free astronomy viewer for FITS images — zoom and brightness stretch.)

Refined Approach
A working principle I've adopted

The native mission FITS is the
only source of truth

Display products — JP2s, PNGs — are stretched, 8-bit, and lossy-compressed. I verified that a JP2 re-wrapped as FITS carries only 256 distinct pixel values versus thousands in the real data. They cannot be used for sub-pixel position work, no matter the file extension.

5Next steps

1

Apply the visual-first workflow to establish a trusted star list per frame.

2

Repeat the brightest-N analysis across many epochs, tracking the same stars frame to frame to pin down the real error budget. This can run on PUNCH as a method rehearsal — but the payoff comes on a finer-scale instrument, since we've confirmed PUNCH itself cannot reach the signal at any radius.

3

Source a science-grade frame from a finer-scale coronagraph. STEREO — at ~15″/px, reaching within a few solar radii of the limb — is the leading candidate. That is where this pipeline points next.
